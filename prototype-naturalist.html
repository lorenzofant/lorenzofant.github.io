About me & Contacts

Lorenzo Fant

Researcher in Theoretical Ecology & Evolution
OGS Trieste  ·  IGC Lisbon

✦

I am a physicist working at the intersection of statistical physics, theoretical ecology, and quantitative data analysis. Since 2024 I am a Researcher at the National Institute for Oceanography and Applied Geophysics (OGS), Trieste, focusing on macroecology and modelling of marine ecosystems. Previously I was a PONTE Post-Doctoral Fellow at Instituto Gulbenkian de Ciência, Lisbon (2022–2024), working on community metabolic scaling and microbial functional ecology, and held a Post-Doc at the Universidad de Granada (2022). I hold a PhD in Physics from SISSA & ICTP, Trieste (cum laude, 2022), with a thesis on stochastic ecology and evolution in bacterial communities.

My research centres on identifying universal principles that organize ecological communities across scales of biological organization. Marine phytoplankton — spanning three orders of magnitude in cell size — are my primary model system: tractable in the laboratory, globally important for primary production, and rich with macroecological patterns that demand mechanistic explanation.

A core theme is the emergence of scaling laws at the community level. In a 2024 paper in Nature Communications, I showed that competitive interactions mediated by biomass density resolve a long-standing paradox between individual- and ecosystem-level metabolic scaling: competition slows metabolism consistently across species of all sizes, making community composition irrelevant to ecosystem-level patterns and unifying physiology with ecology across scales. In work submitted to PNAS (2026), I demonstrate that phytoplankton community dynamics are highly reproducible and quantitatively predictable from demographic parameters measured in simpler experimental settings — yet these parameters show no systematic relationship with cell size, revealing that predictability emerges at the population level and resists reduction to simple trait-based descriptions.

I also study how environmental fluctuations shape evolutionary strategies and life histories. Using stochastic models driven by temporally correlated (colored) noise, I find that environmental autocorrelation generates a non-monotonic fitness landscape for reproductive timing: extreme strategies — very rapid or substantially delayed reproduction — are both evolutionarily viable, while intermediate timescales simultaneously suppress growth and persistence. This "dangerous middle" arises generically from a mismatch between the delay timescale and the autocorrelation of the environment. Related theoretical work addresses how eco-evolutionary dynamics drive communities toward functional robustness and redundancy, and how stable cooperation can emerge as an evolutionarily favoured strategy in stochastic multiplicative growth environments (Phys. Rev. E, 2023).

I was awarded the Young Researcher Prize in the Biodiversity section from Gruppo 2003 per la Ricerca Scientifica (2025).
